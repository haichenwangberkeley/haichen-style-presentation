# Slide contract

Use this contract for routine ATLAS meeting presentations. It defines the minimum structure and the logic of each slide while leaving room for the content and template to determine the exact number of slides.

## Talk-level structure

### Title slide

Include the talk title, presenter, contributors when applicable, affiliations, meeting or venue, and date. Keep a visible slide number if the theme permits it; every slide should be numbered consistently.

### Introductory overview

Set the audience's expectations and state the important message at the beginning. Do not save the punchline for the conclusion. The remaining slides explain, support, and qualify that message.

The introduction normally identifies:

- the overall scientific or technical problem;
- why it matters to the analysis, detector, software, or collaboration;
- what the speaker has done or plans to do;
- the current result, impact, or expected result;
- the organization of the talk, when an outline helps the audience.

### Outline

For every talk with more than five slides after the title, put an outline on slide 2. The outline should preview the main sequence of the talk using a small number of audience-facing topics. Its opening previews the structure, and its closing states where the talk begins. A status slide does not substitute for the outline.

### Main body

Arrange the body around the actual workflow or scientific argument. Include the required record slides for samples, analysis configuration, and machine-learning procedures. A familiar fact may be shown briefly without being discussed in detail.

### Conclusion

Include a concluding slide with the results or current status, a concise summary, and the outlook. Repeat the main message in light of the evidence shown. State unfinished work and next steps precisely.

## The three components of a slide

Every slide must contain the following structure, including title, section-divider, conclusion, appendix, and backup slides:

1. **Visible opening statement:** a concise sentence that previews the rest of the slide. It names the subject and tells the audience what will be described, compared, or checked. Place it below the title and before the main content.
2. **Supporting body:** the method, solution, evidence, or relevant information for that issue. A plot, table, diagram, or short explanation can carry this component.
3. **Visible closing statement:** a concise sentence stating the supported result, impact, limitation, decision, or next action. Place it after the main content and before source lines and footers.

Interpret each component relative to the slide. On a title slide, the opening can state the scientific purpose of the talk and the closing its central message or intended decision. On a backup or record slide, state why the detail is needed and what it establishes. On a section divider, explain the question for the coming section and what it is intended to resolve. Preserve expected or pending qualifiers when there is no completed result.

The two statements must appear as standalone visible text. Titles, captions, source lines, notes, and conclusions inferred from a visual do not substitute for them. Labels such as `Motivation` and `Takeaway`, or decorative boxes, are optional. Generic fillers such as `Results are shown below` do not satisfy the scientific purpose of the rule. The opening must orient the audience before the body, rather than summarize the conclusion in advance.

Use exactly one `\slideopening{...}` and one `\slideclosing{...}` call in each frame, in that order. The commands must render their argument as ordinary visible text. Minimal definitions are provided in [../assets/slide-statements.tex](../assets/slide-statements.tex); adapt their styling to the theme while retaining visibility. Put the calls at frame level, outside columns, notes, conditionals, or visibility commands. Keep them visible throughout overlays. If a frame breaks across pages, each resulting page must have both statements; split it into explicit frames when needed.

The source audit checks explicit frame environments and reports missing, empty, duplicated, and reversed calls as errors. It does not execute TeX or certify rendered visibility. For frames generated by macros, external input files, or repeated-frame commands, provide an assembled source with explicit frames for the audit and inspect the actual PDF; do not claim a complete check from a partial source scan.

Before accepting a slide, answer:

- Why is this slide present?
- What action, method, or solution does it describe?
- What should the audience understand after seeing it?

If an answer or either visible statement is absent, revise or remove the slide. Require 100% opening and closing coverage in both source and rendered review before delivering a compliant deck. Split dense slides or simplify the body to make room; do not shrink or omit these statements.

## Layout

When a slide has a figure or table, prefer explanatory text on the left and the visual on the right. Give the right column enough width for readable labels, legends, and annotations. Use ratios such as 40/60 or 35/65 when the visual needs more space.

Let the figure or table occupy as much of its column as possible. Crop excess margins, enlarge the visual, adjust the column ratio, or move a compact caption before accepting large unused white space. Use a brief caption or nearby comment to tell the audience what to notice.

Use a single-column layout when:

- there is no figure or table;
- the visual is much wider than it is tall;
- a full-width comparison is necessary for readability.

Prefer one legible primary visual over several small visuals. Use multiple panels only when their direct comparison is the point.

## Titles, text, and numbering

- Give every slide a visible, consistently placed number, including title, conclusion, appendix, and backup slides.
- Use a short phrase for each slide title. The title should identify the subject or help carry the message; it should not be a long sentence.
- Use the largest practical text size. Assume some participants will read the deck on a laptop or small remote-meeting window.
- Do not use `\tiny` for substantive slide content. Reserve `\scriptsize` for short source lines, citations, or figure-native annotations; if a table or explanation requires it, split or redesign the slide.
- Keep bullets concise enough to scan while the speaker is talking. Put the explanation in the speaker's delivery when the slide already contains the necessary evidence.
- Avoid unexplained decorative elements and large empty regions.
- Do not use semicolons or em dashes in visible text.

## Figures, tables, and placeholders

- Use figures and tables as evidence, not decoration.
- Give each main figure one specific purpose or claim. Define unfamiliar variables before the figure is shown and make the main result visually dominant.
- Preserve axis titles, units, legends, uncertainty bands, and status labels at readable size.
- Make each scientific figure and table locally interpretable. Define the sample and period, plotted or tabulated quantities, symbols, units, range, normalization, uncertainty or error-bar meaning, curve or colour labels, and applicable numerical criterion when they affect interpretation. Define table columns locally; a glossary elsewhere is not a substitute.
- Distinguish fit errors, signed-MC statistical errors, bootstrap spreads, expected-limit bands, convergence, and physics-validation status. Do not use a generic `uncertainty` label when the distinction matters.
- State why a fit, contour, comparison, or parameterization is being shown. Match the wording of the conclusion to what the figure supports.
- Use a concise public citation when it is needed by the audience. Record the source of numerical inputs and the assumptions that affect interpretation.
- If a required visual is unavailable, use a clearly marked placeholder with a link or precise description of the intended source. Never invent a result plot.
- Remove placeholders before final delivery when the requested source is available.

## Status and privacy

Use the collaboration's correct status terms, including internal, preliminary, work in progress, expected, unblinded, approved, or published. Do not strengthen the status during editing.

Use precise calibration or production identifiers. A label such as `Final` is insufficient when several versions may exist. State whether a signal region is blinded, whether displayed bins have been masked or reset, and which elements remain provisional when those facts affect the figure.

Keep a material scope limitation with the result it qualifies. It should be visible on the introductory status, principal result or interpretation, and conclusion when omitting it there could change what the audience believes was completed.

Keep private source paths, internal production notes, prompts, run directories, and unrequested provenance off slides. Include only the source information needed by the audience or required by collaboration practice.
